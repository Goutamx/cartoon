Cartoons 1–10

1 Princely States and the People

The cartoon shows how princes in the princely states enjoyed luxury while their people suffered. The national leader is asking the ruler to hand over only defence, foreign affairs and communications to the Indian Union. It suggests that the first priority of the government was to integrate the states peacefully, without directly attacking the princes’ social power. The people’s exploitation, however, continued under these rulers for some time.

2 Demands for Linguistic States

Different regional movements like Visala Andhra, Maha Gujarat, Samyukta Karnataka and Brihan Maharashtra are shown demanding their own states. Each group is pulling in its own direction, while the central leader in the middle looks confused and under pressure. The cartoon highlights how strong and emotional the demand for linguistic states became in the 1950s. It also shows how difficult it was for the central government to control or ignore these mass agitations.

3 The ‘Genie’ of Linguism

The big genie represents rising linguistic passions and agitations for separate states. The small man with the bottle symbolises the State Reorganisation Commission trying to control this powerful force. The cartoon says that, like a genie, the politics of linguism could not be easily pushed back once it was released. Leaders watching from behind show how nervous the national leadership was about this issue.

4 Election Committee and Party Tickets

The leaders on the platform represent the powerful party election committee that decides who will get tickets to contest. The crowd below, with folded hands, shows ordinary workers and aspirants trying to please the committee. A man even climbs a tree to attract attention, symbolising how some hopefuls feel completely ignored. The cartoon comments on the lack of inner-party democracy and the centralised control over candidate selection.

5 Tug of War Between Government and Opposition

Here the tree stands for the government firmly rooted in power, with ministers comfortably seated on its branches. Opposition leaders are shown pulling hard at a rope, trying to topple the tree and bring the government down. The large crowd in the background watches this struggle from a distance. The cartoon suggests that, despite the efforts of the opposition, the ruling party in the 1950s remained much stronger.

6 Race for Leadership

The woman leader is already placed on the winner’s stand and is being applauded by party leaders. The runner marked “M” has just started, showing that the contest for top post was unfair from the beginning. The cartoon suggests that the party high command had more or less decided the winner in advance. It comments on how internal elections in the dominant party were heavily influenced by those in power.

7 Non-Communist United Front

The long bicycle represents an alliance of many non-communist, right-of-centre parties trying to move together. The road sign “Keep Right – No Left Turn” shows that they are united mainly by being anti-left and anti-communist. Riding such a loaded cycle on a rough, narrow road looks risky and unstable. The cartoon hints that this United Front was fragile and difficult to manage, even though it tried to present a common alternative.

8 The ‘Left Hook’ in Presidential Election

The victorious boxer stands with a garland, while the boxer wearing shorts marked “Congress” is lying on the floor. A worried leader kneels near the fallen boxer, and a woman politician happily greets the winner. The cartoon refers to the presidential election in which the candidate supported by the Prime Minister defeated the official party nominee. This ‘left hook’ weakened the old party bosses and shifted the balance of power within the Congress.

9 Split Inside the Congress

The woman leader carrying the Congress flag walks firmly ahead, ignoring the three senior leaders behind her. They carry a flag titled “The Indian Syndicate National” and remind her that Gandhi once wished the Congress to be dissolved. The cartoon highlights the clash between the old guard (the Syndicate) and the new leadership over the future of the party. It shows how Gandhi’s name was used in this power struggle, while the new leader wanted to keep Congress as an active political party.

10 The Grand Finish, 1971 Elections

The same leader is shown occupying all three positions on the victory stand—strong, weak and doubtful constituencies. Opposition candidates, still at the starting line, are shocked and say they thought they were ahead. The cartoon explains the 1971 Lok Sabha results, where the ruling party under her leadership swept almost all types of seats. It underlines both the popularity of her campaign and the complete miscalculation of the divided opposition.

Cartoons 11–20

11 Massive Support and Hidden Dangers

This cartoon shows a political leader proudly holding a big trophy labelled “Massive Support”. But inside the trophy there is a dangerous monster, suggesting that huge popular support can also hide the growth of authoritarian tendencies. It is a comment on how unchecked power, even if won democratically, can become harmful for democracy.

12 Central Control over States

Here the central leader is shown fixing and changing the “heads” of leaders from different States as if they are products on a factory belt. The cartoon criticises the practice of the high command deciding who will rule the States, weakening federalism and reducing Chief Ministers to puppets.

13 Economic Crisis of the Early 1970s

The Prime Minister warns that “hard days” are coming, while the common man only hopes that the bad year will pass quickly. The cartoon refers to the economic crisis of the early 1970s, with price rise, unemployment and shortages. It highlights the gap between official slogans and the everyday suffering of ordinary people.

14 The Call to ‘Save Democracy’

On one side we see scenes of corruption, lawlessness and violence; on the other side, a peaceful leader holds a placard saying “Save Democracy”. The rulers accuse him of trying to destroy democracy and grab power. The cartoon refers to the protest movement of the mid‑1970s and shows how governments may label democratic criticism as a threat.

15 Political Crisis before the Emergency

A leader sits firmly on a chair labelled “Conditional Stay Order” while another person tells her that, with so much support, she should not think of resigning. Huge waves marked “Political Crisis” surround them, but the cheering crowd on the shore shouts “Stay”. The cartoon shows how court decisions and party support were used to justify holding on to office even when the political system was in deep crisis, just before the Emergency.

16 Government Not Listening

A leader covers his ears saying, “I can’t hear you!!” to a citizen speaking at a microphone. Around them, huge loudspeakers blast out words like party infighting, probes, exposes, violence and opposition demands. The cartoon suggests that the rulers are so busy with scandals and political battles that they are unable or unwilling to hear the real voice of the people.

17 Fall of the Janata Government

A woman walks proudly with a placard claiming that she alone toppled the Janata government. The leaders of that government angrily reply that it was actually their own quarrels over two years that brought it down. The cartoon underlines that the first non‑Congress government at the Centre collapsed mainly because of internal conflicts and lack of unity.

18 Problems of Opposition Unity

In heavy rain, several leaders struggle to grab one large umbrella, each trying to get under it first. The caption says, “By all means, let’s get under one umbrella, but let me do it first!” The cartoon comments on how opposition parties often talk about unity against the ruling party, but personal ambitions and rivalry prevent real cooperation.

19 Sweeping the Polls

A woman holding a broom walks away confidently, while defeated leaders and their “Vote” placards are crammed into a dustbin. The line “Sweeping the Polls” plays on the double meaning of sweeping with a broom and winning elections by a huge margin. The cartoon shows how one party’s massive victory pushed the opposition into political irrelevance.

20 Risky Path of Economic Reforms

Leaders are shown walking on a weak, broken wooden bridge made of “Loans”, “NRI”, “Foreign Capital”, “IMF” and “World Bank” towards a cliff marked “Welcome to Prosperity”. One leader admits that the path is risky and difficult but promises that all worries will end once they reach the other side. The cartoon is about the economic reforms of the 1990s, highlighting both the hope of growth and the dangers of heavy dependence on foreign loans and agencies.

Cartoons 21–30

21 Support from Outside

This cartoon shows a prime minister being controlled like a puppet by two leaders sitting on top of Parliament. They had promised only “outside support”, but in reality they are pulling the strings of the government. It comments on the weakness of minority governments that depend on outside support from other parties. Such governments are often unstable and heavily influenced by their supporters.

22 1971 vs 1999

The 1971 side shows a very strong Congress leader and a tiny opposition leader bowing down. The 1999 side shows one Congress leader facing a huge tower of many regional and national leaders. It explains how Indian politics moved from Congress dominance to a multi‑party coalition era. The middle quote shows the Congress yearning for the days when it alone dominated politics.

23 Look East / Act East

The cartoon shows an Indian leader near burning files labelled “competition”, with a board of “East Asian nations”. It suggests that closer economic ties with East and South‑East Asia bring very tough competition. The remark “We’ll have to get used to it” means India cannot avoid this challenge. Instead, it must improve its performance to benefit from globalisation.

24 Then and Now in China

The upper panel shows an old communist leader with the little red book, symbolising strict ideology. The lower panel shows a modern Chinese leader in a business suit with a red “power tie”, symbolising capitalism. The cartoon contrasts revolutionary communism of the past with business‑oriented China today. It suggests that China now mixes communist politics with capitalist economics.

25 Great Wall and Dragon

The Great Wall turns into a powerful dragon moving across the world map, showing China’s rising power. The small man in front represents other countries trying to cope with this growth. The cartoon asks whether they can really stop or contain China’s expanding economic influence.

26 Chinese Bicycle

The bicycle marked “China” has one wheel with the communist symbol and the other with a dollar sign. This represents a duality: communist political control combined with a capitalist‑style economy. The cartoon hints that this mix is both a strength and a contradiction in today’s China.

27 Dual Role in Pakistan

The equations on the board use two pictures of the same person – in army uniform and in civilian dress. They show that without either role his value becomes “zero”, but combining both gives “two”. The cartoon says his power depends on holding both army and presidential posts, criticising the military’s dominance over democracy in Pakistan.

28 Balancing Lion and Tiger in Sri Lanka

The leader balances a pole with a lion (Sinhala hardliners) on one side and a tiger (Tamil militants) on the other. It shows the government’s dilemma during the civil war – trying to satisfy the majority while negotiating with rebels. Any move towards one side risks angering the other and upsetting peace efforts.

29 Indo‑Pak Relations

The landscape of bunkers is now filled with countless dialogue tables instead of guns. Indo‑Pak relations have shifted from open warfare to endless, complicated talks. The maze suggests that although dialogue has replaced war, reaching clear solutions remains difficult.

30 SAARC Summit

At the SAARC table, the chairs of India and Pakistan are empty while leaders walk out. This shows how conflicts between these two major members often disrupt SAARC meetings. When they quarrel or boycott, regional cooperation in South Asia comes to a standstill.

Cartoons 31–38

31 External Powers in South Asia

These two cartoons show that issues between India and Pakistan are not decided only by them. In the first, Pakistan wants China to talk about ‘core issues’ with India instead of only trade and commerce. In the second, India and Pakistan are like tennis players while the US sits as the umpire deciding whose side the ball is on. Together they show how big powers such as China and the US strongly influence politics in South Asia.

32 UN and the 2006 Lebanon War

The scene of burning buildings and broken UN helmets shows the heavy destruction in Lebanon in 2006. On the right, the UN Secretary‑General stands trapped inside a cage of barbed wire, looking powerless. The cartoon criticises the UN for acting slowly and weakly, failing to stop the violence and protect civilians. It also suggests that powerful states restrict what the UN and its Secretary‑General are able to do.

33 Darfur – “A Big Helping of Words”

A starving woman and child from Darfur receive a bowl full of words like “aid”, “resolutions” and “never again” instead of real food. Behind them, international officials carry a huge spoon crowded with more speeches and promises. The cartoon says that the world only talks about the Darfur genocide but does little to stop killings or feed refugees. It questions how effective global institutions really are in protecting human rights.

34 Diplomacy or War?

The leader holds a small UN symbol with an olive branch in one hand and a giant iron fist labelled “WAR” in the other. The caption says that if the ‘right’ hand (the UN and peace talks) does not work, the ‘left’ hand of war will be used. The cartoon criticises powerful countries for threatening military action when diplomacy does not give them what they want. It shows the double face of foreign policy—speaking of peace but relying mainly on force.

35 “Taming Peace” and Peacekeeping Forces

Here a fully armed soldier rides a giant dove, which normally stands for peace, and pulls it with reins. The title “Taming Peace” makes this look strange, even paradoxical, for something called a “peacekeeping force”. The cartoon suggests that when peace is controlled by soldiers and weapons, it becomes something imposed by force. It raises the question whether real peace can be achieved mainly through military means.

36 The “Nuclear Powers Club”

Leaders of established nuclear‑weapon states sit comfortably in a “Nuclear Powers Club”, smoking cigars shaped like nuclear bombs. Iran and North Korea also hold such cigars but walk below them, shown as outsiders to the club. The cartoon highlights the double standards of the global nuclear order: some states keep their weapons while others are punished for trying. It asks whether such an unequal and exclusive system can ever be fair or safe.

37 “No Exit” – Money for War, Not for Peace

Citizens request a few million dollars for a new “Department of Peace”, but the politician replies that the country cannot afford it. At the same time, mountains of money are being spent on the Iraq war, the Afghan war, defence and homeland security. The cartoon comments on how governments easily fund armies and weapons but hesitate to support peace‑related programmes. It invites us to think whether our own country also spends more on war than on peace.

38 Globalisation and Jobs – “Yesterday and Today”

In the past, a parent told a child to finish food because people in China and India were starving. Today, another parent tells a child to finish homework because people in China and India are competing for the same jobs. The cartoon shows how globalisation has changed the image of these countries—from only poor nations to strong competitors in the world economy. It suggests that jobs can move across borders and that workers everywhere face new pressure to perform.
